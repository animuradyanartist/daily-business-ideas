## Trend 1: AI-Automated Commercial Financing Disclosure Compliance

**What's happening** — Alternative lenders and merchant cash advance (MCA) providers are adopting AI-powered tools to automate compliance with a growing patchwork of state-level Commercial Financing Disclosure Laws (CFDL). These systems automate the complex calculations and generation of standardized disclosure documents required before funding.

**Why now** — A wave of new state regulations, led by California and New York, is forcing the historically unregulated MCA industry to provide transparent, standardized disclosures. California's rules now require providers of commercial financing under $500,000 to calculate and disclose an estimated Annual Percentage Rate (APR) and other specific cost metrics before a contract is signed. The manual calculation is complex and error-prone, especially for sales-based financing. This has created an urgent need for software that can ingest deal terms, perform the mandated calculations, and generate a compliant, auditable disclosure document as part of the underwriting workflow.

**Forecast (3–6 months)** — The trend will evolve from "disclosure generation" to "agentic" compliance management. An AI agent will be integrated into a lender's CRM and underwriting system. It will monitor all communications with a potential client, automatically generate the required state-specific disclosure at the correct point in the sales cycle, and prevent the deal from advancing until the client's signed acknowledgment is received. The agent will also proactively monitor for changes in state laws, updating its own disclosure logic to ensure continuous compliance.

**Who rides this** — A solo creator could develop "The MCA Broker's Compliance Toolkit." This digital product would be a toolkit for independent brokers navigating these new laws. It would include a multi-state guide to disclosure requirements, Notion templates for tracking compliance for each deal, and pre-written email scripts for delivering the disclosures to clients and obtaining auditable proof of receipt.

**Confidence:** high — This trend is driven by non-discretionary, state-level regulations with significant penalties for non-compliance. The problem is technically complex and operationally painful, creating a clear need for a software solution in a large, fragmented market.

## Trend 2: AI-Assisted Prevention of "Never Events" in Surgery

**What's happening** — Hospitals are deploying AI-powered systems in operating rooms to help prevent "never events" like wrong-site surgery. These tools use computer vision and data analysis to augment and verify manual safety protocols like the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist.

**Why now** — Despite existing protocols, wrong-site surgeries persist due to human error and workflow disruptions. AI systems are now being used to create a redundant, automated verification layer. A study published in July 2026 evaluated an AI system that uses facial recognition to verify patient identity, computer vision to confirm the correct surgical site, and object recognition to authenticate the specific intraocular lens implant. The system significantly increased the detection of near misses compared to the pre-implementation period. Platforms like nSight Surgical and Aimbient use ambient AI to analyze workflows and ensure safety checklists are completed correctly, moving beyond self-reported compliance to objective, data-driven verification.

**Forecast (3–6 months)** — The trend will evolve from "checklist verification" to "agentic" intraoperative safety management. An OR's AI agent will be connected to the patient's electronic health record and real-time surgical video feeds. It will not only verify the patient and site but will also monitor the procedure for deviations from the surgical plan. If the agent detects an unexpected instrument or a step performed out of sequence, it will issue a real-time vocal alert to the surgical team, acting as a vigilant, non-human member of the team dedicated solely to protocol adherence.

**Who rides this** — A solo creator could develop "The Surgical Tech's AI Safety Checklist." This digital product would not be for clinical use but would be a training toolkit. It would include a guide on how current AI systems are being integrated with the WHO checklist, Notion templates for documenting and tracking safety protocol adherence during training simulations, and checklists for validating the setup of AI-powered verification tools in an OR.

**Confidence:** high — This trend addresses a low-frequency but catastrophic-cost event. The financial, reputational, and legal costs of a single "never event" are immense, creating a powerful incentive for hospitals to invest in risk-mitigation technology with a clear ROI.

## Trend 3: AI for Early Case Assessment in Litigation

**What's happening** — Law firms and corporate legal departments are using AI for "Early Case Assessment" (ECA) to quickly analyze the viability of potential lawsuits before committing significant resources. These tools ingest initial case documents and provide a strategic overview of risks, key facts, and potential outcomes.

**Why now** — The cost of litigation is a major driver for adopting AI at the pre-litigation stage. AI platforms can now analyze tens of thousands of documents in hours, identifying critical facts, key themes, and potential strengths or weaknesses in a case. This allows legal teams to make a data-driven decision on whether to settle or litigate before incurring massive discovery costs. Legal tech providers are now offering "Pre-Litigation AI Factfinder" packages, allowing firms to use these tools to investigate potential claims and defenses as soon as a dispute arises. This shifts AI from a tool for managing active litigation to a strategic engine for deciding which cases to take on.

**Forecast (3–6 months)** — The trend will evolve from "case assessment" to "agentic" litigation forecasting. A law firm's AI agent will be given access to a potential client's initial documents. The agent will autonomously analyze the facts, compare them to outcomes from millions of historical court records, and generate a "litigation viability score" that predicts the probability of success, potential damages, and estimated legal costs. This will allow firms to create data-driven fee arrangements and portfolio strategies for their litigation work.

**Who rides this** — A solo creator could develop "The Litigator's AI-Powered Case Intake Kit." This digital product would provide a toolkit for attorneys at small firms. It would include a structured Notion template for organizing initial case files for optimal AI analysis, a library of pre-written prompts for querying AI to extract key facts and identify risks, and checklists for validating the AI's outputs against primary documents.

**Confidence:** high — This trend directly addresses the largest cost and risk factor in litigation: deciding which cases are worth pursuing. The ability to make faster, better-informed decisions at the outset of a dispute provides a clear and compelling ROI for law firms.

## Patterns I noticed today

- The most powerful "why now" is often an external economic force (like a fee change) applied by a regulator to a slow-moving industry.
- A clunky, low-adoption government system is not a threat, but a signal of opportunity. It validates the problem and provides a clear opening for a user-friendly private alternative.
- The perceived risk of a "black swan" event (like an audit or a "never event" surgery) can be a powerful motivator if the consequences are existential (bankruptcy, loss of license) and the preventative action is low-cost.
- Market size can be misleading. A smaller, highly-fragmented market where every single participant feels an acute, legally-mandated pain can be more valuable than a larger market with a diffuse, optional problem.
