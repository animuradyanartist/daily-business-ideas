# The Small Machine Shop's "Real-Time Job Profitability" Tracker

_2026-08-23 · conviction: high · score: 96/100_

## The idea
A simple SaaS tool for small machine shops that provides a real-time dashboard of job profitability. For a monthly fee starting at $99, shop owners get a tablet-friendly interface for machinists to clock in and out of specific jobs, which feeds a live dashboard comparing actual labor costs against the original quote. The core value is replacing "autopsy accounting"—finding out a job was unprofitable weeks after it shipped—with a live view that allows for immediate intervention.

## Who pays and why
The buyer is the owner of a small US-based machine or job shop, typically with 1-10 employees. This owner is obsessed with cash flow and profit margins. They gather on forums like Practical Machinist, in subreddits like r/Machinists, and watch YouTube channels like "Machine Shop Talk". They pay to stop the silent bleeding of money from underbid jobs. A single job that takes 10 quoted hours but actually requires 15 can wipe out the profit from three other jobs, and they often don't have a system to catch this in real-time.

## Problem proof
The pain is the acute, high-stakes anxiety of quoting jobs with incomplete information, leading to direct and often invisible profit loss.

*   **Direct Buyer Quotes:**
    1.  "Quoting is one of the hardest parts of running a machine shop. Quote too high, and you never hear back from the customer. Quote too low, and six months later you're wondering why you're working weekends to stay busy while your bank account doesn't seem to notice." (Uptool on Practical Machinist, June 2026)
    2.  "I've run a few jobs through here... and I've been seeing that the pricing that I've been giving in some cases has been lower sometimes significantly than what the actual costs I have in that job due to the time... and when I've sent those quotes at the new price I'm winning them." (YouTube, Machine Shop Talk, June 2024)
    3.  "...a problem I see a lot and that's when your quotes aren't working out... when the job is done they're not hitting the rate per hour or they're losing money or they're running into issues with their cash flow related to it." (YouTube, Machine Shop Talk, April 2021)

*   **Prevalence, Cost, and Frequency:**
    *   **Prevalence:** The pain is universal to the 17,100+ machine shops in the US. The Practical Machinist forum, a major hub, is filled with discussions on quoting, profitability, and shop management.
    *   **Cost of Failure:** A single underbid job can cost thousands of dollars in lost profit. A systemic issue with quoting can bankrupt a shop. For a shop with a $100/hr rate, just two unbilled hours per day is a loss of over $50,000 per year. Profitability is the primary driver of survival.
    *   **Frequency:** The pain is felt with every single quote sent and every job completed. It is a constant, operational headache.

## Demand signals
Money is actively flowing to complex, expensive, and often poorly-suited software to solve this problem, indicating a strong underlying willingness to pay for a better solution.

1.  **Spend on Enterprise ERPs:** Shops pay thousands per month for complex systems like ECI M1 (starting at $1,000-$3,000/mo) and ProShop ERP (starting at $500-$715/mo) primarily to get a handle on job costing. This proves the pain is worth a significant budget.
2.  **Emergence of Niche Tools:** Companies like Kipware sell one-time license job costing software for $225-$495, proving a market exists for unbundled solutions.
3.  **Spend on Worse Substitute:** The most common substitute is a manual spreadsheet combined with the owner's valuable time spent chasing down numbers after the fact. This "free" solution costs hundreds of hours of a high-value employee's time per year.
4.  **Buyer Intent:** The existence of numerous YouTube channels and forum threads dedicated to quoting and profitability demonstrates active information-seeking behavior from shop owners trying to solve this problem.

**Wallet verdict: PROVEN DEMAND**. The market spends heavily on oversized, complex ERP systems as a poor substitute for a simple, focused job costing tool.

## Why now
Intense margin pressure from volatile material costs and rising labor expenses makes "gut feel" quoting untenable. Survival in 2026 requires a shift from focusing on top-line revenue to managing bottom-line profitability on a per-job basis. The gap left by legacy ERPs, which are too complex and expensive for the vast majority of small shops, creates a clear opening for a simple, modern SaaS tool.

## Unit economics and moat
*   **Pricing:** Tiered SaaS: $99/mo (up to 3 users), $199/mo (up to 10 users), $399/mo for more.
*   **Gross Margin:** >95%, typical for pure software.
*   **CAC/LTV:** Estimated CAC of $3,000 via content marketing and paid search. With a blended ACV of $1,800 and a 15% annual churn, the LTV is ~$12,000. This yields a healthy LTV:CAC ratio of 4:1.
*   **Payback Period:** Approximately 20 months.
*   **Moat Thesis:** The primary moat is **high switching costs created by proprietary customer data**. After a year, the shop's database of hundreds of jobs—with actual vs. quoted times—becomes its single most valuable asset for generating future quotes. This data, created by the customer's own labor, is extremely sticky and difficult to export and import into a competitor's system in a usable format.

## Competitive landscape
1.  **Enterprise ERPs (JobBOSS², ECI M1, ProShop):** They are powerful, all-in-one systems. They do job costing, but it's buried in a complex platform that also does accounting, scheduling, and inventory. Their weakness is their high cost, long implementation time, and complexity, which makes them a poor fit for the target 1-10 person shop.
2.  **Spreadsheets:** The default solution. It's free and flexible but requires manual data entry, is error-prone, and provides no real-time data. It's "autopsy accounting." Its weakness is that it tells you you lost money, but only after it's too late to do anything about it.
3.  **Quoting Tools (Kipware, Uptool):** These focus on the front-end of the problem—generating the initial quote. They help create a better estimate but do not close the loop by tracking actual time against that estimate. Their weakness is that they don't solve the "what actually happened on the shop floor" problem.

## Scope of work
The MVP must be ruthlessly simple, focused only on tracking labor cost against the quote in real-time.

*   **MVP In:** Simple job creation (Job Name, Quoted Hours, Shop Rate), a tablet-friendly machinist interface (list of jobs, big START/STOP buttons), and a single real-time dashboard for the owner showing Quoted vs. Actual hours and costs with a red/yellow/green status indicator.
*   **MVP Out:** No material/tooling costs, no quoting engine, no scheduling, no accounting integrations, no individual pay rates.
*   **Critical Path:** 2 weeks of customer discovery calls with shop owners. 6-8 weeks to build the focused MVP. 2 weeks for onboarding the first 5 beta users.
*   **Resources:** One full-stack developer can build the MVP. Founder-led sales and discovery. Capital required is minimal (<$10k) if bootstrapped.

## Validation plan
The biggest assumption is that a shop owner will pay for a *tracking* tool rather than a *quoting* tool. The test must validate demand for closing the feedback loop.

1.  **Create a "Concierge MVP":** Find one local shop owner. Build their "dashboard" in a shared Google Sheet.
2.  **Manual Data Entry:** For one week, have them text you when a machinist starts and stops a job. You manually update the Google Sheet.
3.  **Review:** At the end of each day, review the "live" dashboard with the owner.
4.  **GO:** The owner says, "This is incredibly valuable, how can I get this for real so I don't have to text you?" and agrees to pay $99 for the real version.
5.  **REFINE:** The owner says, "This is interesting, but I really need X to make it useful."
6.  **PAUSE:** The owner says, "Thanks, this is neat to see but it doesn't change my decisions." This indicates the pain is a vitamin, not a painkiller.

## Sales playbook
1.  **Pricing strategy:** Launch at $99/mo for up to 3 users, $199/mo for up to 10. Grandfather early adopters. Tier justification is based on the number of people logging time.
2.  **Top 3 sales channels:**
    *   **Content Marketing (YouTube):** Create short videos analyzing common quoting mistakes, using the software as the solution. Title: "You're Losing Money on 30% of Your Jobs. Here's How to Find Them."
    *   **Forums (Practical Machinist):** Participate authentically in shop management threads. Don't spam. Offer insights and, when relevant, mention the tool. "A lot of guys use spreadsheets, but we built a simple tool to see this in real-time because we kept getting burned."
    *   **Direct Outreach:** Find 100 shops on Google Maps. Send a one-line email: "Subject: Job Costing. Do you know your real profit on a job before you ship it?"
3.  **Hook copy:**
    *   **A:** **Headline:** Stop Guessing. Start Knowing. **Sub-headline:** The real-time job profitability dashboard for small machine shops. See which jobs are making you money—and which are costing you—while they're still on the machine.
    *   **B:** **Headline:** Is this job profitable? **Sub-headline:** Finally, a simple way to see your actual labor costs versus your quote, in real-time. No more waiting until the end of the month to find out you lost money.
4.  **Pre-launch → launch → sustained:**
    *   **Pre-launch (Day -30):** Announce development and a "Founder's Circle" beta on Practical Machinist.
    *   **Launch (Day 0):** Publish a detailed case study of the first paying customer.
    *   **Sustained (Day 30+):** Begin weekly YouTube content schedule.
5.  **Top 2 buyer objections:**
    *   "My guys won't use it." → "It's two taps on a tablet. If they can use a smartphone, they can use this. It's simpler than a digital caliper."
    *   "I can do this in my spreadsheet." → "You can, after the fact. Can your spreadsheet alert you the moment a job goes over budget, while you can still do something about it?"
6.  **Social proof strategy:** The most powerful proof is a screenshot of the dashboard showing a real (anonymized) job that was saved from unprofitability, with a quote from the owner.

## Red-team and rebuttal
*   **Attack:** This is a vitamin, not a painkiller. Owners care about winning the *next* job, not doing an autopsy on the last one.
    *   **Rebuttal:** This isn't an autopsy; it's a real-time diagnostic. The data from this tool is the *only* way to make sure the quote for the next job is actually profitable. It directly solves the "win the next job" problem by making the quoting process intelligent instead of a guess.
*   **Attack:** The moat is a puddle. This is a simple CRUD app that can be cloned in a weekend. An incumbent like JobBOSS can add this feature in a single sprint.
    *   **Rebuttal:** The moat isn't the code; it's the customer's accumulated historical performance data. After 500 jobs are logged, that dataset becomes the shop's proprietary quoting intelligence. Migrating that is a massive pain. Incumbents won't add it because their business model is built on complexity and high-touch sales; a simple, self-serve tool cannibalizes their core offering.

## Risks and kill criteria
1.  **Adoption Failure:** Machinists refuse to use the system consistently, rendering the data useless. Kill if <70% of job hours are logged after a 30-day onboarding with a pilot customer.
2.  **"So What?" Syndrome:** Owners see the data but it doesn't lead to any change in behavior or improved outcomes. Kill if 3 pilot customers say it's "nice to know" but not "need to have" after 60 days.
3.  **Incorrect Pricing:** The $99-$199/mo price point is too low to build a real business or too high for the value provided. Kill if the LTV:CAC ratio is projected to be less than 3:1 after the first 20 customers.

## What I learned today
- A pain doesn't have to be driven by an external regulator (like the FAA or FDA) to be acute. The internal pressure to remain profitable can be an equally powerful, if not stronger, purchase driver.
- The biggest competitor to a simple SaaS tool is often not another piece of software, but a manual, "good enough" process. The sales job is to quantify the hidden cost of that manual process.
- In a market dominated by complex, expensive incumbents, the most powerful wedge is not more features, but radical simplicity and a focus on solving the single most expensive problem.
- A product's moat can be built from the customer's own operational data, which they create through the act of using the product, leading to powerful switching costs.
