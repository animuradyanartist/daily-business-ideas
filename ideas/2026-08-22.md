# The Apprentice Electrician's "Get-My-License" Hour & Category Tracker

_2026-08-22 · conviction: high · score: 92/100_

## The idea
A software-as-a-service (SaaS) product for apprentice electricians to track, categorize, and verify the 8,000+ hours of on-the-job training required for a journeyman license. The mobile-first app translates complex, state-specific work category requirements into a simple daily logger, preventing errors that can delay licensure by months or years. The price is a simple subscription, around $15 per month.

## Who pays and why
The buyer is an individual apprentice electrician in the United States, typically in year 1-4 of their training. There are an estimated 324,000 such apprentices at any given time. They gather in online communities like the `r/electricians` subreddit and watch trade-specific YouTube channels.

They pay because their future earning potential is directly gated by the accurate logging of these hours. A journeyman license can increase their annual income by $20,000 or more. State licensing boards have complex rules, often requiring hours to be logged across up to nine specific work categories (e.g., commercial, industrial, residential). Manually tracking this on a spreadsheet is tedious and error-prone. A single mistake in categorization can lead to a rejected application, delaying their license and costing them thousands in lost wages.

## Problem proof
The pain is a direct, multi-year administrative burden with severe financial consequences for failure, proven by state regulations and user testimony.

*   **Direct buyer quotes:**
    1.  **YouTube Comment (Dec 2025):** "many successful apprentices use a two-tiered tracking system this means keeping both the official state required log and a personal more detailed journal... consistent verifiable documentation is your strongest ally in this process." This shows apprentices are already doing double work because official systems are inadequate.
    2.  **Reddit `r/electricians` (Aug 1, 2026):** "I’m an apprentice in WA state... I use an app called Timesheet. It works great for tracking my hours but I have to manually separate my hours into the 16 different categories." This demonstrates active use of generic tools and the specific pain of manual categorization.
    3.  **Reddit `r/electricians` (Feb 14, 2026):** "Is there an app that you guys use to track your hours? I'm trying to find one that I can use to track my hours and have my journeyman sign off on them." This is a direct request for the proposed solution.

*   **Prevalence:** Most states mandate 8,000 hours of on-the-job training over 4-5 years. Washington state, for example, requires apprentices to log hours across 16 distinct categories and have a minimum of 4,000 hours in commercial/industrial work. California requires tracking across 9 categories. This complexity is widespread.

*   **Cost of Failure:** The cost is a delayed pay raise. Apprentice electricians earn a median of ~$45,000/year, while licensed journeymen earn a median of ~$67,000/year. A one-year delay due to a logging error represents a direct financial loss of over $20,000.

*   **Frequency:** The pain is constant. Apprentices must log their hours daily or weekly for 4-5 years straight.

## Demand signals
Money and effort are already flowing to solve this problem, indicating a high willingness to find a better solution than paper or spreadsheets.

1.  **Active Search for Solutions:** Threads on forums like `r/electricians` repeatedly ask for app recommendations for tracking hours, demonstrating active, unsolved pain.
2.  **Use of Generic Substitutes:** Apprentices are already using and paying for generic time-tracking apps like Timesheet and then manually adapting them, proving a willingness to pay for software to solve this.
3.  **Emergence of Niche Competitors:** The existence of apps like WireHours and "Electrician Apprentice Hours" on app stores, while basic, proves that others have identified this pain and that apprentices are downloading dedicated tools.
4.  **High Spend in Adjacent Markets:** Airline pilots, who face a similar career-gating logging requirement, pay significant fees for specialized logbook software. LogTen Pro charges up to $129.99 per year, proving that professionals pay for high-stakes record keeping.

**Wallet verdict: PROVEN DEMAND**. The market is actively using and paying for inferior substitutes and downloading purpose-built (if basic) tools, while adjacent professional markets prove a high willingness to pay for this exact type of solution.

## Why now
1.  **Increasing Regulatory Complexity:** State licensing boards are continually refining requirements. Washington state's rules were updated as recently as July 2026, and Alabama's in February 2026. This increasing complexity makes manual tracking more difficult and risky, driving demand for a tool that stays current.
2.  **Digital Native Workforce:** The new generation of apprentices entering the trades are digital natives who expect a software solution for administrative tasks. They are less likely to tolerate paper-based systems their predecessors used.

## Market structure
The market is a large, fragmented base of highly motivated individual buyers facing a mandatory, career-gating administrative task.

*   **TAM/SAM/SOM:**
    *   **TAM:** The US skilled trades industry.
    *   **SAM:** There are ~81,000 new electrician job openings per year. With a 4-year apprenticeship, this implies a constantly replenishing market of **~324,000 electrical apprentices**. At $149/year, the addressable market is **~$48M annually**.
    *   **SOM (Year 3):** Capturing 1% of this market (3,240 apprentices) × $149/year = **~$482,760 ARR**.
*   **Growth:** The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects electrician employment to grow 6% from 2022 to 2032, faster than the average for all occupations, ensuring a steady stream of new apprentices.
*   **Fragmentation:** The buyer base is hundreds of thousands of individuals working for thousands of different contractors. A low-cost, direct-to-consumer model is a perfect fit.
*   **Margin pool:** The value is captured by accelerating an apprentice's path to a ~$20,000/year pay increase. A $149/year tool that de-risks this transition offers a massive ROI.
*   **Incumbents:** The main incumbent is the spreadsheet. Existing apps are basic, often free or ad-supported, leaving a clear gap for a premium, professional-grade tool.

## Unit economics and moat
*   **Pricing:** $15/month or $149/year. This is a trivial expense compared to the ~$20,000/year salary increase it protects.
*   **Gross Margin:** Pure SaaS, so margins are ~90%.
*   **CAC/LTV:** With acquisition through targeted social ads and content marketing, a CAC of $50 is achievable. The average user will subscribe for the duration of their apprenticeship (assume 4 years). LTV = 4 years × $149/year = $596. This yields a healthy **LTV:CAC ratio of ~12:1**.
*   **Payback Period:** $50 / ($15/mo * 0.9) = ~3.7 months.
*   **Capital intensity and time-to-first-revenue:** Low. An MVP could be built by a solo developer in 3-4 months.
*   **Moat thesis:** The primary moat is **switching costs created by user data**. After two years of logging hours and collecting supervisor e-signatures, the verified, auditable record becomes too valuable and difficult to migrate. The product becomes the system of record for the user's career progress.

## Competitive landscape
1.  **Spreadsheets/Paper:** The dominant but flawed incumbent. It's free but error-prone, offers no validation against state rules, and is easy to lose.
2.  **WireHours:** A direct competitor. It has the right feature set (state rules, supervisor verification). This validates the market. It leaves a gap by having a less professional brand presence and a freemium model that may signal a less robust product.
3.  **Generic Time Trackers (e.g., Timesheet):** Users are already adopting these but find them lacking the critical work-category tracking specific to their trade. This proves willingness to use an app but highlights the need for a specialized solution.

## Scope of work
The build is a straightforward mobile-first web application.
*   **MVP In:** User accounts, daily hour entry form (with fields for date, hours, work category, notes), dashboard showing progress against state-specific requirements, digital supervisor sign-off workflow, PDF export of logs. Must launch with rules for the 5-10 largest states (CA, TX, FL, NY, etc.).
*   **MVP Out:** Team features, invoicing, advanced reporting, exam prep modules.
*   **Milestones:**
    *   Month 1: Database schema, user auth, basic hour logging form.
    *   Month 2: Implement rule engines for 5 states, build progress dashboards.
    *   Month 3: Supervisor sign-off logic, PDF export, payments integration.
    *   Month 4: Beta testing with apprentices, polish UI/UX, launch.
*   **Resources:** One full-stack developer. No significant capital required beyond hosting and standard SaaS tools.

## Validation plan
The biggest assumption is that apprentices will pay $15/month instead of using a free spreadsheet or a free/cheaper app.
1.  **Test:** Build a landing page that details the features, emphasizing "peace of mind" and "avoid costly delays." Target ads on Facebook and Instagram to users with "Apprentice Electrician" as a job title or interest.
2.  **Channel:** Facebook/Instagram ads, posts in `r/electricians` (if rules permit).
3.  **Metric:** Drive traffic to a waitlist signup for an "Early Adopter" plan at $99/year (a discount on the $149 list price).
4.  **Thresholds:**
    *   **GO:** >5% visitor-to-waitlist conversion rate. If 100 people sign up for a paid waitlist, the demand is validated.
    *   **REFINE:** 1-5% conversion. The pain is real, but the messaging or price point may be wrong. Survey the waitlist.
    *   **PAUSE:** <1% conversion. The willingness to pay is not there. Re-evaluate.

## Sales playbook
1.  **Pricing strategy:** Launch at $15/month or a discounted $149/year. Lock in early adopters at this price for life. Raise prices to $19/month or $199/year after the first 1,000 users.
2.  **Top 3 sales channels:**
    *   **Reddit:** Engage authentically in `r/electricians`. Answer questions about licensing, and only mention the app when someone directly asks "how do you track hours?"
    *   **Facebook/Instagram Ads:** Target users aged 18-30 with job titles/interests like "Apprentice Electrician," "IBEW," "Electrical Trainee."
    *   **Content Marketing/SEO:** Create a definitive guide for the licensing requirements in all 50 states. "How to Get Your Journeyman License in Texas," for example. Funnel visitors from the guide to the product.
3.  **Hook copy:**
    *   **Version A (Fear-based):**
        *   Headline: Don't let a spreadsheet error delay your license.
        *   Sub-headline: Track your apprentice hours, get supervisor sign-offs, and generate board-ready reports that are 100% compliant with your state's rules.
    *   **Version B (Benefit-based):**
        *   Headline: The fastest path from apprentice to journeyman.
        *   Sub-headline: The #1 hour tracking app for electricians. Log your hours, meet your state's requirements, and get licensed without the paperwork headache.
4.  **Pre-launch → launch → sustained:**
    *   **Pre-launch (Day -60 to -1):** Build the 50-state licensing guide content. Run waitlist ads. Engage in forums.

5.  **Top 2 buyer objections:**

6.  **Social proof strategy:** The most powerful proof is testimonials from newly licensed journeymen. "I just got my license and the board accepted my reports from [App Name] with no questions asked." Feature these prominently on the homepage with a photo of their license.

## Red-team and rebuttal
1.  **Attack:** The market is transient. You have to replace your entire customer base every 4-5 years.
    *   **Rebuttal:** Correct. The business model is not based on lifetime retention but on becoming the default intake tool for the constant stream of ~81,000 new apprentices who enter the trade each year. The "market" is this annual flow, not a static pool.
2.  **Attack:** A spreadsheet is good enough. This is a vitamin, not a painkiller.
    *   **Rebuttal:** This misreads the motivation. The pain isn't the annoyance of data entry; it's the five-figure financial risk of getting it wrong. The product isn't a convenience; it's insurance against a catastrophic career delay. For 50 cents a day, it eliminates the single biggest administrative risk an apprentice faces.

## Risks and kill criteria
1.  **Risk: Inability to acquire customers cost-effectively.** Paid acquisition might be too expensive to support a $15/month price point.
    *   **Kill criteria:** If after spending $5,000 on ads, the cost to acquire a paying customer (CAC) is over $100, the unit economics are broken. Stop.
2.  **Risk: The "good enough" problem is real.** Despite the logic, apprentices may still prefer a free, manual solution.
    *   **Kill criteria:** If the landing page test shows a <1% conversion rate to a paid waitlist, the willingness-to-pay assumption is false. Stop.
3.  **Risk: Technical complexity of state rules.** Maintaining 50 different rule engines is more complex than it appears.
    *   **Kill criteria:** If after building for two states, it becomes clear that the edge cases and rule variations make scaling to all 50 states untenable for a small team, the model is not viable. Stop.

## What I learned today
- A user's personal, career-gating financial milestone can be as powerful a motivator as a B2B compliance fine.
- The best moats are often built from the customer's own tedious work, which they then become unwilling to repeat in a competitor's product.
- A transient customer base isn't necessarily a bad business if the inflow of new customers is large, predictable, and constant. The business becomes about optimizing acquisition for that flow.

## Why this beats yesterday's idea
Yesterday's idea, the small brewery's keg tracker, was strong because it solved the pain of direct, recurring asset loss (~$150 per lost keg). This is a clear ROI. Today's idea is stronger because it's tied not to preventing a small loss, but to unlocking a huge, life-changing gain: a ~$20,000/year salary increase. The emotional and financial leverage is significantly higher for the apprentice. While the brewery owner is motivated to save costs, the apprentice is singularly focused on achieving this one career milestone over a multi-year period. This focus creates a more intense and less price-sensitive buyer.
